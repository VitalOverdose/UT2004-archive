11/24/04

I think I realized something very important tonight, something that could very easily have changed my life. And as much as it pains me to think about it (literally, it pains me – I get major headaches from this depth of circular thinking)....

Tonight, as I was pursuing a recently attained interest of mine (creating and using my own programming language), I had a particularly difficult time deciphering a technical document on compiler construction. I thought nothing of it at first. But, as experience has taught me time and time again, I usually underestimate the amount and difficulty of effort required to take on my "snap-projects", as I call them.

Then it hit me. It'll probably be easier to describe with the aid of an extremely short list of my goals:

  • Finish PD mod for UT
  • Develop a commercial computer game from scratch

So you're thinking, "Two things, that's it?!" No. That's only an extremely short list, or rather, the part of the list that I'm concentrating on right now. Well, actually, the second item is really a spur-of-the-moment type thing. I haven't slept on it yet.

So what?

I've been really intense into computers for about...10 years, now. And I expect I'll be just as (if not more) intense as the years come and go. I'll say right now that I wish every year lasted for 1,000 years, just so I could do everthing I want to do...

Anyways, back to my underestimation of projects. A couple times before (and now I realize that it makes no difference what they were), I've taken up projects on impulse alone. After what I thought would be the extent of time period of work, I realized that I had to settle for a half-assed version of what I had originally wanted. Not cool. After about three or four of these "incidents", I realized that by sleeping on an impulse, about 90% of the time when I woke up in the morning, I would find a hole in my expectations, or a flaw in my process, and so on, and so on. Therefore, I could save a lot of wasted time and effort just by giving myself one night to consider my impulse. Incidentally, the last time I had to use this method was deciding on whether or not to pursue the PD mod (I eventually became experienced enough to recognize bad ideas without needing time to think it over).

What's this all leading up to? Direction, that's what. Screw some long-winded philosophical discussion. Right now I need to narrow my focus. Tonight I realized that, hard as I try, I cannot do 20,000+ things at once (by rough estimate). I need to focus on one thing at a time (or 1000, a 95% reduction). *sigh*....

If you know me just a little, you'll know that I don't care so much for the result as I do the process. And now I'm saying that the result overshadows the process....? Holy shit, what the fuck is happening to me?!

No way...

Come Hell or high water (or any other major disaster), I shall not abandon my principles, nor my huge list of goals and projects. I will prevail !

More importantly.... I am the physical manifestation of determination, the very embodiment of the human will. There is no stopping until it is finished. In short, I WILL NEVER GIVE UP.

11/19/04

I was coding at a pretty good rate the other night (don't inquire as to what night I am talking about). Things were running unbelievably smooth, and I was feeling pretty good. I had just finished coding up the primary fire of the CMP150 for my Perfect Dark mod. Then all hell broke loose. Well, not really, but that's kind of what it seemed like.

I compiled all the code without trouble. Then, when I entered UT to test out my new weapon, it just....stopped. The screen was black, no mouse or keyboard input, not even the last-resort Ctrl+Alt+Del worked. Okay, so I powered down the entire system. Cold-boot, and back into WOTGreal.

I quickly isolated the problem to the weapon I just created (well duh....everything worked fine the night before). More specifically, the sound import statements were the cause. Here's what's running through my head when I figure that out: "WTF? Why would sound cause a system-wide crash?" So I commented them out. Sure enough, recompiled, started UT again, and away it went.

So I exited UT and dug in a little more. Went over the sound imports in detail to check for misspellings or missing files. Nope, no errors. Then deleted and redid the sounds to check for file corruption. Recompile, run UT. Another crash. By now, I'm getting really pissed because it takes about 2 minutes to cold-boot.

This went on for the rest of the night, trying out different things and code-scouring and crashing. Suffice to say, I was not in a good mood when I went to bed.

Next morning, I tried again. Another crash. But then, something clicked. "Alright, I tried redoing the sounds, why not just resample them?" "But wait, all the other weapons don't have sound problems, and they're all the same, 22kHz, 8-bit mono." "Just try it." (I often talk to myself) So I resampled the sounds to 22kHz, 8-bit stereo. This time, I get a different problem, one that doesn't require me to cold boot, and it's more informative, too. Something about "channel not found" or something. So I'm thinking, "Okay, I know UT doesn't like stereo sounds." So I resampled again, this time 22kHz, 16-bit mono. It worked.

I can't describe the amount of profanity that escaped my body through the whole ordeal. So once again, I'll leave with a bit of advice: check your sound samplings. I still don't know why UT wouldn't take 22kHz, 8-bit mono for that one time.

10/25/04

It is really hard to clean up somebody else's code.

I already knew this, but the full meaning didn't sink in until I started to recover code from a pre-BetaV2 release (of my PD mod). Okay, so this is turning out to be a reminiscence of earlier days, but I still feel that I need to say it anyways.

To all coders out there: please, for the sake of everyone else, USE A STANDARD. What you do in one thing, you should do it the same way in a similar thing, or maybe use a common parent class so you only have to write it once.

No matter how many comments you have, not using a standard is....well, similar to the following metaphor: Let's say that the code is a sheet of paper, and that your comments are writing on that paper. If you're a good coder, you keep every sheet nice and flat (metaphor for standards), using a bright white so the black ink of your comments stands out. If you're a bad coder, you crinkle every sheet (metaphor for lack of standards), and use a dark-grayish color so that you can barely see your black-ink comments. Now....which piece of code would you rather read and interpret, the nice, clear, smooth code, or the wrinkled, smudged (and maybe even ripped ), and inscrutable code? Now imagine the people that have to deal with that "bad" code. It is extremely hard for them to smooth out "bad" code. Usually they have to take a new sheet of code and start over.

Luckily, code isn't written on paper, so we don't quite have the extremity of that situation.

Okay, that's the end of my lecture on code clenliness.
